What the panel composition is telling you

Spend two minutes on this before anything else. It reframes how you should answer everything that follows.

The frame that should govern this entire round

Under the SFC's Manager-In-Charge of Core Functions regime, a licensed corporation must file with the SFC the name, job title and reporting line of the named individual in charge of each of eight core functions. The SFC's own example job titles for the Finance and Accounting function are "Chief Finance Officer; Financial Controller; Finance Director." Its example titles for Operational Control and Review include "Head of Operations." confirmed

So it is very likely that at least two of the three people you are meeting are named on a document at the SFC as personally in charge of the functions your requirements would change. In client-asset matters the SFC has said explicitly that senior management "including responsible officers and Managers-In-Charge of Core Functions, bear primary responsibility." confirmed

What follows from that. When the Head of Finance asks "how do I know this number is right?", that is not finance pedantry — it is a person asking about their own licence. When the Head of Dealing asks what happens to a rejected order, they are not testing trivia. Answer every question in this round as though the person asking will be held personally accountable for your answer, because they may well be. Say the phrase "so you can evidence it" more than once. It is the single most reassuring thing an operations owner can hear from a BA.

Client taps Buy in the app

Platform
What happens
Order ticket built: instrument, market, side, quantity, order type, price. Board lot validated for HK. Buying power checked against available to trade. Product eligibility and entitlement checked — is this client, in this entity, with this investor classification, allowed to trade this instrument?
What the BA specifies
Which balance figure funds the order, and whether unsettled proceeds count. Complex-product gating and warning screens where the instrument is flagged complex. Board-lot and odd-lot handling. For virtual assets, the pre-funding rule and per-client exposure limit fire here, not later.

2

Pre-trade risk and regulatory validation

Dealing owns the rules
What happens
Automated controls reject or hold the order: price-band and erroneous-order checks, credit and exposure thresholds, market state (halted, auction, closed), and the regulatory checks — a valid BCAN must accompany a HK order or it is rejected outright; Northbound sell orders are pre-trade-checked against the previous day's position; Connect accepts limit orders only.
What the BA specifies
The exception queue — and this is the most under-specified area in most retail apps. What is auto-rejected versus held for the desk, what the client sees and when, and how a held order is released without jumping ahead of orders received later. Also: what the app does when the exchange gateway throttle is exhausted on a busy open.

3

Routing and execution

Dealing
What happens
Straight-through to SEHK for HK. For Korea, a venue choice exists between KRX and the Nextrade ATS. For the US, almost certainly via an overseas executing broker. For virtual assets, to the partnered SFC-licensed platform. Fills return, partial or full.
What the BA specifies
Order-status model and client messaging for partial fills, expiry and rejection. Session-aware order types per market. Whether the client is told which venue filled them. The best execution obligation stays with the Hong Kong licensed entity even when an affiliate or third party executes — so the monitoring artefacts are a requirement, not an afterthought.

4

Trade capture, enrichment and the contract note

Middle OfficeFinance cares
What happens
Execution is booked and enriched from static data: settlement market and currency, counter, stamp-duty exemption flag, fee and levy schedule. Charges are computed — commission, stamp duty, SFC levy, AFRC levy, exchange trading fee, clearing fee. A contract note must be issued within two business days, with charges disclosed separately, not blended.
What the BA specifies
The fee engine and its rounding: stamp duty rounds up to the nearest dollar; levies and the trading fee round to the nearest cent; each computed separately before summing. Rates must be configurable and effective-dated with an audit trail — three of them have changed since 2023. The app is the delivery channel for a legally prescribed document, so the confirmation screen is a compliance surface.
↓
5

Clearing — netting into a settlement obligation

Settlement
What happens
For HK, HKSCC becomes the counterparty by novation and the trade enters the CNS system, where positions net same-day and across days. A Final Confirmation Statement of the firm's actual net obligation arrives around 2pm on T+1. This is why depository movements never equal the day's trades.
What the BA specifies
An expected-net-position layer as the reconciliation key — reconciling client-level trades directly against depository movements will never tie. This single point, said out loud, will tell the Middle Office head that you have actually thought about their job.
↓
6

Settlement — stock and money, on different clocks per market

SettlementFinance funds it
What happens
HK settles T+2 through CCASS batch runs against a designated bank account. Northbound settles stock T-day but money T+1, in RMB only. Korea is T+2 in KRW with cash typically pre-provisioned a day or two ahead. US is T+1, and the FX leg has to settle before the security. Client money must sit in a segregated trust or client account.
What the BA specifies
The balance matrix: settled cash, unsettled receivable, unsettled payable, on-hold, pledged, blocked for corporate action — by market and currency — and which UI figure each cell feeds. Withdrawal, buying power and margin all derive from one place, or they diverge. Plus four settlement calendars and an FX calendar, as configurable data.
↓
7

Reconciliation and exception resolution

Middle OfficeFinance signs
What happens
At the end of each business day the firm's own trade records are matched to the clearing house lists and to counterparty and executing-broker confirmations. Client money and client securities positions are reconciled against bank and depository statements. Fails are chased — an unsettled short HK position after the final run on T+2 can be borrowed against or bought in from T+3, at the failing party's cost.
What the BA specifies
Break reporting and ageing, and the exception workflow with maker-checker. The recurring break causes are worth knowing: netting, omnibus opacity where the depository sees one position, fee rounding, corporate-action timing, multi-counter securities, and FX rate source or timing mismatches.
↓
8

Books, records, client reporting and regulatory returns

Finance
What happens
Commission recognised as revenue; levies remitted; the firm's own earned commission must be moved out of the segregated account promptly. Monthly client statements go out where client assets were held at any point in the period. The firm files monthly Financial Resources Rules returns built from trading and back-office data, and must maintain liquid capital above its requirement at all times.
What the BA specifies
Data lineage — which new or changed field lands in which return and which statement. Statement generation completeness controls. And the sweep of the firm's own commission out of client money, because if that is manual or monthly it is a breach accruing daily rather than an accounting inconvenience.

Interviewer 1

Christina Ho — Head of Finance

Her underlying question is "can I trust the numbers this app produces, and can I trace them?" She is very likely the Manager-In-Charge of Finance and Accounting, which means timely and accurate financial reporting is her named regulatory responsibility. In a Hong Kong subsidiary of a foreign parent, the Finance head typically also carries regulatory reporting, client money oversight, payment authorisation, group reporting to the parent, tax and audit liaison. typical practice, not a rule

Know these four things and you will hold this conversation comfortably

1 · Liquid capital. Under the Financial Resources Rules a licensed corporation must maintain liquid capital — broadly, liquid assets less ranking liabilities — at or above its required liquid capital, at all times, not at month-end. confirmed

2 · The return is built from trading data, not the general ledger. The monthly Financial Return is a set of forms covering margin client balances, securities collateral, cash client balances and total client assets. So a change to how the app books a trade, accrues a fee, holds a pending balance or classifies a client lands in a regulatory return. confirmed

3 · The clause that should change how you write requirements. A firm that becomes aware it is unable to maintain — or unable to ascertain whether it maintains — required financial resources must notify the SFC and cease regulated activity. A broken data feed into the capital computation is itself a notifiable event. confirmed

4 · Client money has a one-business-day rhythm. Client money must reach a segregated trust or client account within one business day of receipt — and money in a segregated account that is not client money, such as the firm's own earned commission, must be paid out within one business day. The firm's own money cannot sit there as a buffer. confirmed

"The app calculates fees and charges. How would you test that it's right?"

The highest-probability question in this conversation, and the one where specificity wins outright.

"I'd recompute independently of the system rather than testing the system against itself — build the expected charges from the published schedules and compare. Then I'd concentrate the test cases on the rounding and the boundaries, because that's where fee engines actually fail. Stamp duty rounds up to the nearest dollar per side; the SFC and AFRC levies and the exchange trading fee round to the nearest cent; and each has to be computed and rounded separately before you sum them. Get the order of operations wrong and every ticket is out by cents, which at retail volume becomes a real remittance variance. I'd also test the minimum-commission floor, tiered-rate boundaries, and any FX conversion, and I'd want the rates held as effective-dated configuration with an audit trail rather than hard-coded — three of them have changed since 2023."

Then offer the three you know changed, because it demonstrates you checked rather than assumed: the HKEX trading tariff of HK$0.50 per transaction was abolished on 1 January 2023 when the ad valorem fee rose to 0.00565%; the CCASS settlement fee minimum of HK$2 and maximum of HK$100 were removed in June 2025 for exchange trades, which matters most at exactly the small ticket sizes a retail app generates; and stamp duty was cut to 0.1% in November 2023. Also say AFRC levy, not FRC levy — the body was renamed, and using the old name signals stale reading. all confirmed, but re-check the HKEX fee page the day before

"How do you make sure a change to the app doesn't break our reporting?"
"By treating data lineage as part of the requirement rather than something Finance discovers afterwards. Practically, for any new or changed data element I'd want to answer three questions before the spec is signed off: which report or return does this land in, who is accountable for that number, and can they trace it back to the transaction without asking a developer. I'd rather come to you with 'this new field affects these two returns, please confirm the treatment' than ask you to check whether the numbers look right after go-live."

Why this lands. The SFC's 2024 circular on financial resources management found firms had "inadequate or ineffective controls over liquid capital monitoring" and failures to make proper accruals, and it expects a maker-and-checker mechanism for the calculation, monitoring and reporting of liquid capital. confirmed Offering maker-checker unprompted is speaking her language. Reinforce with your own evidence: you verified end-of-day data feeds for trade settlement and post-trade surveillance with Risk and Reconciliation — that is exactly this discipline.

"Have you actually worked with a finance function before, or just with product and technology?"
"Yes, and in two different modes. Operationally, I led the UAT that verified our end-of-day data feeds for trade settlement and post-trade surveillance, working with Risk and Reconciliation — so I've been on the hook for whether downstream numbers tied out. And on the investment side, I've written the business cases and ROI models that went to the executive committee, so I'm used to defending a number to people whose job is to challenge it rather than to like it."

Don't overclaim accounting knowledge. The credible position is: you are fluent in data and in business cases, not in FRR mechanics — and you know which questions to bring to her rather than answer yourself.

"How do you prioritise between something the business wants and a control the app is missing?"
"I try not to frame it as revenue against control, because that argument is always won by whoever is louder. I frame it as cost of delay against cost of failure — what the feature is worth per month versus what the control failure costs if it happens, including the regulatory and remediation cost, not just the direct loss. For anything with a client-asset or reporting consequence I'd want it sequenced ahead of a revenue feature, and I'd want that decision recorded rather than assumed, so nobody has to reconstruct the reasoning a year later."

The phrase "recorded rather than assumed" matters to someone with named accountability. It says you will leave an audit trail of the decision, which protects her.

"What do you need from my team, and when?"

A generous question and an easy one to fumble by asking for too much. Answer with respect for her calendar.

"Mostly two things, and both early rather than often. First, sign-off on treatment — for each new data element, which report it lands in and how it should be classified. Second, someone to review the reconciliation and exception design before build, not after. And I'd want to book those away from your close and reporting windows — month-end, the return deadline, quarter-end group reporting. I'd rather work around your calendar than land a requirements review in the middle of it."

Knowing that her month has spiky, immovable deadlines — and saying you will plan around them — is a small thing that buys disproportionate goodwill. It signals you have worked with a finance function that had real deadlines.

"Do you know what a segregated account is?" / client money probing
"At the level a BA needs, yes — client money sits in an account designated as a trust or client account with an authorised institution, it has to get there within one business day of receipt, and money in there that isn't client money has to come out within one business day. The part I'd flag as a product design question rather than an accounting one is the second half: if the app is debiting commission from clients, then the sweep of our own earned commission out of the segregated account is a systems process. If that's manual or runs monthly, it isn't an accounting inconvenience — it's a breach accruing daily. So I'd want to know whether that's automated today."

This is your best single question for her. A real enforcement case involved a firm failing to move roughly HK$38m of its own commissions and interest out of segregated accounts within one business day over about two and a half years, alongside unauthorised transfers, resulting in a reprimand and a HK$2.5m fine. confirmed, firm anonymised in the source You don't need to cite the case. You just need to show you know the fee engine is a client-money surface, not only a revenue surface. Very few BAs know that.

Interviewer 2

Wind Chan — Head of Dealing, Wealth Management

His underlying question is "will your requirements break my market access, or leave my desk holding a mess?" The "Wealth Management" qualifier matters: he likely deals for advised and relationship-managed clients as well as self-directed flow, which means suitability, order recording and funds/bonds dealing are all in his world, not just listed equity execution.

The one fact that anchors this entire conversation

Hong Kong's best execution rule is one sentence. Code of Conduct paragraph 3.2, in full: "A licensed or registered person when acting for or with clients should execute client orders on the best available terms." That is the whole paragraph. confirmed verbatim

All the substance sits in the SFC's best execution circular of January 2018 and its accompanying thematic review. The two things from it to know cold: firms need a written execution policy covering the factors considered, any carve-outs, the monitoring mechanism, staff roles and client disclosure — and "the obligation to deliver best execution remains with" the licensed corporation even where orders are routed through affiliates or third parties. confirmed

Why this is the anchor. Hong Kong is a principles-and-supervision regime, not a disclosure regime — there is no public execution-quality report to publish. The SFC wants to open your execution committee minutes, your venue review and your monitoring output during an inspection. For a BA that means the deliverable is evidence artefacts: reviewable monitoring reports, exception logs, documented periodic venue reviews. Say that and you have understood his problem.

"If our Korean or US flow is executed by an affiliate or a local broker, who owns best execution?"
"We do — the Hong Kong licensed entity. As I understand the SFC's position, the obligation stays with the licensed corporation even where orders are routed through an affiliate or a third party, so 'our Korean broker handles the routing' isn't an answer we can give. What we'd need to be able to evidence is that we selected that broker deliberately, that we review them periodically, that we know whether they route across both KRX and Nextrade or default to KRX, and that we monitor the resulting execution quality. And if we do route to a single venue for a market, my understanding is that can be a legitimate carve-out — but it has to be documented in the execution policy and disclosed to clients, not an undocumented accident."

This is the highest-value answer on this page for him. It is also where he is most likely to press, because it is genuinely unresolved: Nextrade trades roughly 650 KOSPI and KOSDAQ names and runs to 20:00 KST, well past the KRX close, and at least one international broker went live smart-routing across both venues in mid-2026. confirmed So a follow-up like "what does our app do between 15:30 and 20:00 KST when Nextrade is open and KRX isn't?" is very likely. The honest answer is that it is a product decision — reject, queue to next KRX open, or route to Nextrade — and each option carries a different disclosure consequence.

"Can we cancel a fat-finger trade in Hong Kong?"

A test question. Anyone from a US market background gets this wrong.

"Essentially no, for cash equities. My understanding is that trades concluded on SEHK aren't amended or cancelled — bids and offers are firm — and the only route is reporting an error to the Exchange where the Board is satisfied it wasn't intentional, which is a narrow exception rather than a review window. There's no US-style clearly-erroneous regime, so in practice the broker wears the loss into an error account and fixes it with a compensating market trade. Which is exactly why I'd argue pre-trade validation deserves sprint capacity in Hong Kong more than it would in the US — the exchange isn't going to save us. Derivatives are different, with a short filing window and a review panel, but that's a separate mechanism."

Why this answer is worth rehearsing. It does three things at once: gets a counter-intuitive fact right, draws the correct HK-versus-US contrast, and converts it into an argument for spending build effort on controls. That last move is what a Head of Dealing wants from a BA. substance confirmed; describe by title rather than quoting rule numbers

"What happens to an order that fails validation?"

He asks this because it is his actual daily workload and most BAs have never specified it.

"The exception queue, and I'd treat it as a first-class part of the design rather than an error path. Every control generates rejects — invalid or missing BCAN, price outside the permitted band, board-lot violations, insufficient buying power, market halted or in auction, gateway throttle exhausted. So I'd want to specify three things: what is auto-rejected versus held for the desk; what the client sees and how quickly, because a silently held order generates a call; and how a held order is released. That last one is the part people miss — the prompt-execution and 'handle orders in the order received' obligations keep running while an order sits in a queue, so if you release a held order after later orders have already gone to market, you've created a fairness problem out of a technical one."

Paragraph 9.1 of the Code requires a firm to "handle orders of clients fairly and in the order in which they are received," and 3.1 requires prompt execution in accordance with instructions. confirmed verbatim Connecting the exception queue to order-sequencing fairness is a genuinely uncommon insight and the single best thing you can volunteer to him.

"What does the app do when it goes down?"
"The desk becomes the channel, which makes it your problem rather than a technology problem — so the requirements aren't only about systems. Concretely: a published fallback number and a client communication that fires automatically rather than being drafted during the incident; surge capacity on the phones, because an outage on a volatile day produces calls from clients who have never phoned before; enough licensed representatives on shift in every time zone we cover, because a fallback channel without a licensed rep isn't a fallback channel; telephone recording live on the fallback lines, since an outage is exactly when taping gaps appear; and manual orders still going through the same pre-trade validation, which means a desk-side entry tool with the same checks rather than a naked terminal. Then the recovery problem: reconstructing which orders were accepted, which were lost and which got duplicated on reconnect. Duplicate-on-recovery is the classic retail failure."

The licensed-representative point is the one almost no BA raises, and it turns a systems contingency into a headcount and licensing constraint. inference, well-grounded Note also that the SFC's 2022 online-brokerage review criticised inadequate capacity planning and contingency arrangements for keeping client services uninterrupted. confirmed

"Where does suitability get signed off when a relationship manager calls in an order?"
"That's the architectural question I'd want to resolve early, because the same app is serving two regimes. Suitability attaches to a recommendation or solicitation, so a genuinely self-directed order isn't in scope — but an RM-originated order for a wealth client is. Which means the order has to carry its channel: the desk needs to be able to see, before execution, whether this order came through a self-directed path or an advised one, and if advised, that there's a suitability reference attached to it. If the answer today is 'the RM confirms verbally', that's a gap. If the order carries a reference the desk can check, that's a designed system."

A strong answer, and it opens onto his order-recording world. Worth knowing: the Code requires client order instructions received by telephone to be recorded and retained for at least six months — notably shorter than the European standard, so don't assume five years — and separately requires firms to prohibit staff from taking client order instructions on mobile phones at the usual place of business, with a written policy to enforce it. confirmed verbatim That mobile prohibition is a live control problem in any wealth business with hybrid working, and raising it unprompted is a strong signal.

"When would you escalate a client's trading activity?"
"Early, and on suspicion rather than proof. My understanding is the notification threshold is deliberately low — we don't need to identify the specific breach or work out the mechanism before reporting. And on what to actually monitor, I'd start where Hong Kong's real risk is, which isn't high-frequency spoofing — it's ramp-and-dump in thinly traded small caps run through networks of retail accounts. So the surveillance backlog I'd propose starts with linked-account detection and profile-versus-activity mismatch: groups of clients sharing contact details or operators and trading in sync, transaction sizes out of line with the client's stated profile, end-of-day purchases lifting the close in illiquid stocks, and large sales followed immediately by withdrawal requests."

Those red flags come almost directly from the SFC's 2021 circular on notifying suspected ramp-and-dump scams, which states the reporting threshold "is low" and lists exactly these patterns. confirmed One precision point: spoofing and layering are not named in the SFO — they are prosecuted through the false trading and price rigging provisions. Don't say "the SFO prohibits spoofing"; say those provisions are the hook and spoofing is a surveillance typology. He will notice the distinction.

"Do you know what our throttle allocation is?"
"No — but I know why you're asking. Throttle is a purchased, finite resource at the exchange gateway, and a retail app that fires a burst of orders at the open on a hot day can exhaust it, at which point orders queue or get rejected at the gateway rather than by us. So 'what does the app show a client when we're throttle-limited' is a real requirement, and buying more throttle is a cost line rather than a config change. What's our current allocation, and have you hit the ceiling?"

For context you don't need to quote: one securities-market throttle rate allows about two messages per second, with a setup fee and a monthly charge per rate, and China Connect has a separate allocation. confirmed Admitting you don't know the firm's number while showing you understand the mechanism is a better answer than guessing.

"How do you handle Connect orders differently?"
"Several ways that all become app rules. Northbound accepts limit orders only, so if the app's default order type is market, it either has to be hidden or converted for Connect — and that's a UX decision with a disclosure consequence. Sell orders are pre-trade checked against the previous day's position, so 'sellable quantity' has to be a market-specific field based on the T-1 close rather than the live position. No day trading, so what was bought today can't be sold today, and that needs saying in the interface rather than surfacing as a rejection. Daily quota can exhaust intraday, which means a state where buys stop but sells continue, and a message a client can actually understand. And Northbound closes when Hong Kong is closed even if the Mainland is open, so the trading calendar has to be data, not code."

All confirmed from HKEX materials. confirmed The calendar point is the one to land: across four markets plus a continuously traded crypto book, the trading calendar becomes a first-class data asset the desk owns and the app consumes — and it is the most commonly under-built component in multi-market retail apps.

"The US overnight session starts in December. What does that mean for us?"
"The interesting thing is that it lands in our daytime. Nasdaq's new overnight session runs 21:00 to 04:00 New York time, which is roughly 10:00 to 17:00 Hong Kong in winter — so it isn't a night shift, it's additional US market activity during hours the desk is already fully staffed on Hong Kong, Connect and Korea. The genuine coverage gap is the evening seam, after Korea closes and before US regular hours open. There are two specific app consequences I'd want on the backlog: market orders and market-on-close reportedly won't be available overnight, so if the app's default is a market order the session silently breaks it; and the trade-date convention splits at midnight New York time, which is a settlement and reporting requirement rather than a desk concern."

Confirmed from Nasdaq's own trader alert for a 6 December 2026 start, including the 21:00–04:00 session, the ±20% price bands, orders being cancelled at 04:00, the trade-date split, and order-type restrictions. confirmed But it is explicitly pending SEC rule changes and market-data readiness, so say "targeted" rather than "will."

"Why don't our depository positions match the day's trades?"

If they ask an opening test question, there's a good chance it's this one.

"Netting. In the CNS system positions net not just within the same settlement day but across days — unsettled positions carry forward and net against opposite positions later. So stock movements at the depository will never equal the sum of the day's client trades, and reconciling those two directly is a design mistake. You need an expected-net-position layer in between, calculated the way the clearing house calculates it, and reconciled against the confirmation statement. Which as I understand it arrives around 2pm on T+1 today and would move to T-day evening under the T+1 proposal."

Same-day and cross-day netting, and a Final Confirmation Statement issued at or about 2pm on the business day following trade date, are both confirmed from HKSCC operational procedures. confirmed This single answer is probably the strongest thing you can say to this person.

"What's the difference between available to trade and available to withdraw?"
"They're three different numbers, and conflating them is the most common defect in retail brokerage. Available to trade may include unsettled sale proceeds, because the firm is effectively financing settlement timing. Available to withdraw has to exclude anything not irrevocably received. And unsettled proceeds themselves behave differently per market — Hong Kong T+2, Northbound stock on T-day but money on T+1, Korea T+2, US T+1, and crypto effectively pre-funded and immediate. The way I'd specify it is a balance matrix: rows are balance types — settled cash, unsettled receivable, unsettled payable, on-hold, pledged, blocked for a corporate action — columns are markets and currencies, and then define per cell which UI figure it feeds. Withdrawal, buying power and margin then all derive from one place instead of drifting apart."

The balance-matrix framing is the kind of concrete deliverable an operations head can immediately see the value of. And it ties back to the fact that on a combined traditional-and-digital-asset app, crypto must be pre-funded while equity proceeds are not — so "available to trade" genuinely means different things in the same account.

"How would you approach corporate actions?"
"As a deadline model rather than a spreadsheet, because there are four layers and they're in different time zones. The market or depository deadline, the custodian's deadline with their own internal buffer, our internal cut-off, and then our client-facing cut-off, which has to sit earliest. All stored as timestamps with time zone, all configurable per market rather than hard-coded, plus a default election and an audit trail of who applied it. And once a client submits an election, the holding has to be blocked — which loops straight back to the balance matrix. In Hong Kong the depository's own deadlines are unforgiving and set at noon on the relevant day, so the buffer isn't theoretical."

HKSCC nominee-service deadlines for deposits and elections are at 12:00 noon on the relevant day, and cash entitlements received after a mid-afternoon cut-off credit the next business day. confirmed Note the Northbound wrinkle if it comes up: HKSCC is the nominee holder in omnibus accounts at ChinaClear, so elections pass through two intermediaries.

"What causes most reconciliation breaks, in your experience?"
"In my experience the recurring ones were static data and timing rather than anything exotic — an instrument set up slightly differently in two places, or a rate or cut-off applied on different days. From what I understand of a multi-market operation, I'd expect the pattern here to be netting, fee and levy rounding, corporate-action timing where cash credits land on a different date from the accrual, multi-counter securities where the same stock trades in more than one currency, and FX rate source or timing mismatches between the booking rate, the settlement rate and the valuation rate. And the hardest one structurally: wherever we hold an omnibus position, the depository sees one number and client-level truth exists only in our own books — so an internal error has nothing external contradicting it. That's the case for independent client-balance confirmation on a sample basis, which I gather the SFC has pushed on."

The omnibus-opacity point is genuinely sophisticated, and it is exactly why the SFC has pressed firms on independent client-balance confirmation. confirmed Lead with your own experience, then mark the rest clearly as reasoning rather than practice.

"How would you handle a client who sells in one market to buy in another?"
"That's the case I'd want to model explicitly, because it's where the settlement calendars collide. Take a client selling a Korean stock and buying a US stock the next day — Korean proceeds are T+2 in won, and converting won to dollars may only complete on a New York business day, while the US buy needs dollars for T+1 settlement, and the FX leg has to settle before the security. So either we finance the gap or we block the trade, and that's a commercial decision with a funding cost, not a technical default. What I'd want is for the rule to be explicit and disclosed rather than emergent — the worst outcome is a client discovering the constraint at the point of a failed settlement."

Cross-currency, cross-market funding timing is one of the genuinely hard problems of a four-market retail app, and framing it as a commercial decision with a funding cost rather than a systems limitation is the mature answer. FX-before-security under T+1 confirmed

"Hong Kong is moving to T+1. What would that mean for this app?"
"It compresses the exception window to nothing, which is the real impact. As I understand the April 2026 consultation, implementation is indicatively Q4 2027, the final clearing statement moves from T+1 afternoon to T-day evening, instruction input and matching windows extend into T-day night, and — the one I'd flag hardest — the T+1 trade amendment window is eliminated, so error correction becomes same-day only. Which means middle office stops being a next-morning resolution function and becomes a same-day one. For the app, that pushes everything upstream: better pre-trade validation because there's less room to fix things afterwards, earlier FX triggers, and automation of anything currently done by hand in the evening. It's a multi-year programme rather than a release."

All confirmed from the HKEX consultation paper. confirmed Say "indicatively Q4 2027" — it is a proposal, not a commitment. This is a strong closing note because it shows you can see their two-year problem, not just the current sprint.

The question to ask them, and it's the best one on this page "Which access model are we on for the US — an omnibus account with a US clearing broker, or fully disclosed?" It determines who owns client-level reconciliation, who allocates corporate-action proceeds, who handles the tax documentation, and whether we have an intraday view of US positions at all. It is the fastest way to show you understand that the operating model drives the requirements, not the market. Worth asking the same question about Korea — direct, via a Korean affiliate, or through the omnibus structure that opened up for foreign retail this year.
All Three

Cross-cutting questions

"You're a BA. Why should we trust you near our production configuration?"
"Because I've been on the other side of it. The multi-asset platform I delivered came with a back office, so I've worked in that layer — fee groups, the user-to-exchange matrix, trading currencies, mid FX rates, product whitelists and blacklists. Which means I know that the risk isn't the change, it's the change nobody can trace afterwards. So the first thing I'd want to do is map every place a product or parameter has to be configured, and find out whether the same configuration is replayable from UAT into production or re-keyed. Re-keying is where 'it worked in test' incidents come from."
"How do you handle it when operations tells you a requirement isn't feasible?"
"I try to separate 'this can't work' from 'this can't work the way you've described it', because they need different responses. Usually the second is true and the answer is in the detail — a cut-off I didn't know about, a manual step I'd assumed away. So I go and watch the current process rather than argue from the spec. My habit is to map the as-is end to end before proposing anything, which is slower at the start and much faster overall, because it stops me designing around a constraint that doesn't exist and into one that does."
"Tell me about a requirement you got wrong."

Have a real one ready. It should be a requirement that was right on paper and wrong in operation — that's the failure mode this panel cares about. Structure: what you specified, what actually happened at go-live or in the first weeks, how it was found, what you changed in your process afterwards. Avoid a story where the failure was someone else's, and avoid one where the fix was "I was more careful." The credible fix is a process change — a review step you added, a person you now always consult, a category of test case you now always write.

"How would you spend your first month?"
"Learning the product as an operator rather than a user — every journey, but also every place configuration lives and every hand-off between teams. Concretely I'd want three things by the end of it: a map of the configuration layer and how many places a new instrument or market has to be set up; the top defect and drop-out themes from actual data rather than opinion; and a list of the manual steps each of your teams does that exist because a system doesn't do them. That last list is usually where the highest-value work is, and it's also the fastest way to earn credibility with the people doing those steps."

That third item is aimed directly at this panel. Asking operations people which manual workarounds they'd most like removed is both genuinely useful and the single most goodwill-generating question a new BA can ask.

"How do you write a specification that operations people will actually read?"
"Short, and structured around the process rather than the system. I use as-is and to-be process maps so people can see their own job in it, and I write acceptance criteria as scenarios rather than as feature descriptions — including the exception paths, because that's the part operations cares about and the part specs usually leave out. And I'd rather walk someone through a draft in twenty minutes than send a fifty-page document, because the questions they ask in that twenty minutes are the requirements I missed."
"Why are you leaving your current role?" (expect this again)

Same answer as round one — scope, not escape. You've been the senior BA on someone else's platform strategy; this is a chance to lead the BA function for a platform at the start of its life, in a market you know. With this panel you can add a line that lands well with operations people: that you want to be somewhere the whole lifecycle is in one building, close enough to the people running it that you can go and watch the process rather than reading about it.
