The Distinctive Ask

"Product administration setup" — what they're actually asking for

This phrase appears in both the responsibilities and the requirements. It is the least standard thing in the JD and therefore the most likely to be the reason the role exists. Most candidates will read past it. Don't.

The reframe that wins this A "BA who also does product administration setup" is a BA who owns the configuration layer between the requirement and the running system — the place where a business decision becomes a live parameter. On a retail trading app that layer is enormous, mostly invisible, and almost always the source of the worst production incidents. Say that out loud. It shows you know why they wrote the bullet.

What sits in that configuration layer on a retail trading app

Generic and illustrative — a framework for the conversation, not a claim about Mirae Asset's actual architecture. Say so if asked directly.

Product / instrument master

Which instruments exist, their market, currency, board lot or minimum size, tick size, trading hours, corporate-action handling, and the complex vs non-complex flag that drives the regulatory journey. Getting one flag wrong changes what a customer legally has to see before they trade.

Entitlements

Which client, in which legal entity, with which investor classification and which risk profile, can see and trade which product. This is where jurisdiction, professional-investor status, derivatives-knowledge assessment and product-eligibility rules all collide.

Fee, commission and FX schedules

Per-market commission tiers, platform fees, custody fees, FX conversion spreads, promotional overrides and their expiry. Frequently the highest-volume change request in a new app, and the one most likely to be done under time pressure.

Operational rules and limits

Order-type availability, price-tolerance and fat-finger checks, position and exposure limits, buying-power and settled-vs-unsettled cash rules, withdrawal limits and cut-off times.

Market and calendar configuration

Trading calendars per market, settlement cycles, holiday handling, corporate-action and dividend timing. On a multi-market app this must be data, never hardcoded — a point worth making explicitly.

Release-related setup

Feature flags and phased-rollout cohorts, content and disclosure versions, minimum-app-version enforcement, and the configuration that must be staged in production before a release is switched on. This is the "release-related setup activities" bullet, literally.

The follow-through worth preparing — manual configuration as an automation target

You said it yourself: if parameters are being updated by hand, that's where the time and the error rate live. Have a concrete version of that argument ready, because on a six-week-old app it is very likely to be true and it's the most credible improvement you can offer.

The shape of it: manual configuration fails in three predictable ways — it isn't replayable (what was set in UAT gets re-keyed in production, so "it worked in test" stops meaning anything), it isn't auditable (you can see the current value but not who changed it, when, or why — a real problem when the regulator's expectation is maker-checker on high-impact actions), and it doesn't scale with the roadmap (every new market multiplies the same manual steps). The fix isn't necessarily a big automation programme: it's usually configuration-as-data with an approval workflow and an export that can be diffed between environments. Framing it that way shows you'd propose something proportionate rather than a rebuild.

Domain Knowledge

The Hong Kong regulatory layer a retail trading app runs on

You don't need to be a compliance officer. You need to know which SFC obligations turn into screens, fields and gates in an app — because that is precisely a BA's job, and it's a topic most BA candidates cannot discuss concretely.

1 · Internet trading security — the most prescriptive rules, and the most app-visible

  • The SFC's Guidelines for Reducing and Mitigating Hacking Risks Associated with Internet Trading set 20 baseline requirements. Two-factor authentication for client login took effect 27 April 2018; the remainder on 27 July 2018. confirmed
  • Mandatory client notifications — on system login, password reset, trade execution, fund transfers to non-registered third-party accounts, and changes to client particulars. Critically, the notification channel must differ from the login channel. Clients may opt out of trade-execution notifications only after risk disclosure. This is a concrete app requirement with a concrete design consequence. confirmed
  • The SFC's 2020 thematic review added specifics that read like a mobile spec: 2FA must be non-bypassable; no email-based OTP; brokers should not allow an excessive number of bound devices and should understand and assess the reasonableness of a request to bind more than three devices (an enquiry trigger, not a hard cap — don't state it as a limit); limit the idle timeout period, e.g. within 30 minutes, subject to assessment, and don't let clients disable session timeout; block rooted or compromised devices; obfuscate source code; purge sensitive data on logoff; annual self-assessment against the baseline. confirmed
  • A February 2025 circular encouraged licensed corporations to move away from SMS OTPs toward biometrics or software tokens, and to encrypt data at rest as well as in transit. A June 2026 circular on AI-enabled cyberattacks added least-privilege access, micro-segmentation, maker-checker for high-impact actions, treating external inputs as adversarial, and prompt notification to the SFC of material cybersecurity incidents. confirmed
The one-month-old change that should be in this conversation

On 9 July 2026 — five weeks ago — the SFC issued a circular to licensed internet brokers and SFC-licensed virtual asset service providers mandating phishing-resistant authentication. Firms must stop using one-time passwords for client login and device binding altogether; the SFC's stated position is that an OTP is not a phishing-resistant solution. The transition is to passkeys or bound devices, with a deadline of 8 July 2027, and large internet brokers are expected to move sooner rather than use the full window. confirmed by multiple independent reports incl. Hong Kong press and law-firm briefings; I have not read the circular text itself

Why this is the single best card in your hand. MAPS is a brand-new internet trading app that also carries a virtual-asset offering — so it sits squarely in scope on both counts, and it launched before this circular landed. There is a real chance nobody has written the requirements for it yet. The crypto layer — Hong Kong's retail virtual asset regime

Not a JD requirement. But if the app is MAPS, this is the most operationally distinctive thing about it, and the area with the most live regulatory change. Knowing this cold is how you go from "well-qualified" to "obviously prepared."

"Where don't the two halves fit together?" — prepare this properly, because you've invited it

Your Beat 2 line about having delivered both halves separately and knowing where they don't fit neatly is the best hook in your introduction. It's also a promise. Here is the substance to back it, in rough order of how hard each one is:

One balance, two funding models. Crypto orders under the omnibus model must be pre-funded. Equities settle T+2 in Hong Kong and T+1 in the US, so a client can have buying power that isn't cash yet. Putting those behind a single "available to trade" figure is a genuine requirements problem — do unsettled equity proceeds fund a crypto order? Almost certainly not, and the customer will not find that intuitive. Somebody has to design the explanation, not just the rule.

Two custody models, one portfolio view. Equities sit in a securities account under client-securities rules; virtual assets sit in segregated accounts with a licensed platform or authorised institution. The app shows them in one portfolio. What the client believes they own, and what they legally own, need to line up in the disclosure.

Continuous versus session-bound markets. Crypto trades 24/7; equity markets have sessions, calendars and holidays that differ per market. Overnight price moves have no equivalent on the crypto side — which affects valuation timing, statement cut-offs, and any margin or collateral logic touching both.

Two gating regimes at onboarding. The derivatives-knowledge characterisation and complex-product journey on the securities side, the VA knowledge test and exposure limits on the digital side — potentially two separate assessments, two pass/fail states, and a real question about whether the client experiences it as one onboarding or two.

Reconciliation and irreversibility. An equity break gets investigated and corrected; an on-chain withdrawal doesn't come back. That asymmetry is exactly why the February 2026 circular requires robust authentication, real-time monitoring and 24/7 incident response specifically on client VA withdrawals.

You don't need all five. Two, delivered calmly, will do more than a list.

Lead with the constraint, not the feature list

Anyone can list AI features. What separates a BA Lead is knowing which ones are legal to ship in Hong Kong and in what order. Two facts do most of the work:

1 · The SFC has already designated AI investment advice as high-risk. The SFC's Circular on the use of generative AI language models (12 November 2024) designates using an AI language model to provide investment recommendations, investment advice or investment research as a high-risk use case. That triggers extra requirements: a human in the loop reviewing output for factual accuracy before it reaches the client, testing that semantically identical prompts don't produce different answers, and disclosing that the client is interacting with AI and that output may be inaccurate — every time they interact, not once at onboarding. Under the Guidelines on Online Distribution and Advisory Platforms, robo-advice explicitly triggers the suitability requirement, and the SFC's own FAQs draw the line precisely: "the posting of factual, fair and balanced product-specific materials would not in itself amount to a solicitation or recommendation" — but a list presented as matching "your risk tolerance level" does. confirmed

The one-sentence version to say out loud: "The moment client profile data influences which instruments get surfaced, you're inside the suitability regime — so the sequencing question is which AI features stay on the objective side of that line, and which ones are worth paying the full suitability and algorithm-governance cost for."

The Delivery Rhythm

Mobile release management — what's different about shipping an app

The JD asks for "release notes" and "release-related setup activities," and for driving delivery through "launch readiness, and post-launch review." Mobile releases behave differently from web releases in ways that are worth being able to name.

1

You cannot roll back an app release

Why it matters
Once a build is in the App Store or Play Store and customers have installed it, you can't recall it. The only real remedies are a forward fix (subject to review timelines) and server-side feature flags or remote configuration.
What a BA owns
Making sure every material new feature ships behind a flag with a defined off-state that degrades gracefully — and that the off-state has actually been tested, not just designed. This is a requirements decision, not a purely technical one.
↓
2

You support many versions at once

Why it matters
Customers upgrade at their own pace. At any moment you have several app versions in the wild talking to one backend — so every API change needs a compatibility story, and every disclosure or risk-warning change needs to reach clients on old versions too.
What a BA owns
The minimum supported version and forced-upgrade policy, and the rule for which changes are regulatory-material enough to require a forced upgrade rather than a soft prompt. On a regulated trading app that is a compliance decision wearing a product costume.
↓
3

Config must land before the switch flips

Why it matters
A release is rarely just a build. It's a build plus product parameters, plus entitlement changes, plus fee schedules, plus content and disclosure versions — often across several systems, sequenced, in production, under time pressure.
What a BA owns
The release-readiness checklist that turns "we deployed" into "we can safely switch it on," and the go/no-go criteria agreed with Ops and Compliance in advance rather than argued about on the night. This is the JD's "release-related setup activities," and it's where your PMO governance instinct is a direct asset.
↓
4

Post-launch review has a regulatory edge

Why it matters
Beyond the usual adoption and defect metrics, a regulated trading app must report material system failures to the SFC under the Code of Conduct's notification requirements (para 12.5, "Notifications to the Commission"), and material cybersecurity incidents promptly under the cybersecurity circulars. Separately, the hacking-risks Guidelines require contingency planning covering DDoS and ransomware; and the SFC's August 2022 online-brokerage review criticised inadequate review of system capacity and contingency plans for keeping client services uninterrupted. confirmed — three separate instruments, don't merge them
What a BA owns
Defining what counts as "material" before it happens, and making sure the incident, client-communication and notification path is documented rather than improvised. Six weeks after launch, this may well not exist yet — which makes it a credible thing to offer.
Precision warning Code of Conduct para 12.5 ("Notifications to the Commission") is safe to cite — it's verified in the 33rd edition (January 2026) and does require immediate reporting of system failures among other events. What is not safe is the Schedule 7 sub-paragraph numbers for the electronic trading regime, or the record-retention period — those could not be verified. Refer to "Schedule 7 of the Code of Conduct on electronic trading" by title only. General rule for the whole interview: nobody penalises you for not citing a sub-paragraph; citing one wrongly is far worse than not citing it.

Anticipate

Likely questions & framing notes

"Tell me about your experience with retail trading platforms."

Your strongest question — don't rush it. Structure: what the platform was (retail multi-asset, web and mobile, with a back office you worked in), what you specifically owned (requirements and journey design for the CFD-only → multi-asset expansion, across multiple legal entities), and one hard problem you solved end to end. Answer in three moves: first, find out what's actually broken — defect themes, support ticket categories, onboarding drop-off, not opinions. Don't answer with process theatre. Honest differentiation, not disparagement: a local broker BA will know cash-equity plumbing better than you. "What would your first 90 days look like?"

Have this ready as a real answer, because for a lead role they will ask. You're moving from Senior BA to BA Lead — a step up in scope but arguably lateral in title terms.
