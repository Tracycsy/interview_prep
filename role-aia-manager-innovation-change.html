THE OPENER

Your self-introduction (≈ 1 min 45 sec)

Built for this role's fit story: identity → the craft, evidenced → the enterprise-scale move → the AIA-specific hook. Speak it, don't recite it.

Beat 1 · Identity · ~20 sec

"Thank you — I'm Tracy, PMP and Professional Scrum Master certified, with eleven years across Hong Kong's betting and financial services industries. My craft is turning technology and process change into something an organisation actually uses — through governance, communications, training and measurable adoption. Five of those years have been in finance, at FXCM; the earlier six were at the Hong Kong Jockey Club, where I ran large-scale customer-facing platform rollouts. Different industries, same discipline."

Beat 2 · The current-role frame · ~25 sec

"My current role at FXCM is planning enterprise adoption of Claude AI and Jira/Confluence Cloud tooling across engineering and business teams. That means going project by project — understanding what each Jira team actually does, identifying where AI can automate real work, and designing the governance, RACI and KPI framework before rollout — so when we launch, we're driving adoption of something people actually need. It's early-stage work, but the shape — governance-first, evidence-driven, adoption-planned — is exactly what this role scales up."

Beat 3 · Training & adoption at scale · ~45 sec

"Where I have the deepest track record is the training, communications and adoption side of change — enterprise rollouts across multiple locations, formats and audiences. On FXCM's investment platform expansion, I ran the enablement programme across our overseas offices: on-site workshops for local teams, virtual training for distributed users, live demo sessions, and the user guides and FAQs that let people self-serve after launch. At the Hong Kong Jockey Club I ran the same playbook for the launch of new betting terminals across branches and for our AI-enabled mobile onboarding app — and I also handled the upstream communications piece: hosting a townhall to preview a strategic project's upcoming changes to the wider business, and presenting at area meetings to bring the message directly to frontline teams. Different products, different geographies, same discipline: making sure the people using the change hear about it, understand it, and actually adopt it."

Beat 4 · The AIA bridge · ~25 sec

"What draws me to this role is the chance to apply the same craft at genuine enterprise scale — five of AIA's largest markets, on the back of TDA and now the shift into embedded generative AI. Innovation change management across Hong Kong, Mainland China, Singapore, Malaysia and Thailand isn't a project, it's a multi-year discipline coordinating five mature markets with strong local views, and I'd like to spend the next chapter of my career on it. AIA's focus on 'healthier, longer, better lives' is a mission I'd genuinely want my work to serve — and the Group Innovation Office is where change actually gets driven into those markets."

WHY THIS SHAPE WORKSBeat 2 is honest about where the AI work actually is (planning, not delivered) while showing governance-first thinking — a much stronger signal than overclaiming shipped work. Beat 3 is now the load-bearing beat: enterprise training and adoption across FXCM's investment platform and HKJC's terminals and onboarding app — the most direct evidence you have for the JD's Training & Enablement duties. Beat 4 answers "why us" with three specific AIA references (scale, TDA/genAI, mission). It never mentions FXCM instability — that's an internal reason, not an interview reason. If they ask directly why leaving now, the answer is "seeking greater enterprise scale and career depth" — true, and forward-looking.
Delivery notes

Numbers are load-bearing. HKD 100M+ betting terminal programmes, 300K-client migration, three-person BA team, 30% ticket resolution improvement — every figure comes from your CV, be ready to defend each with two sentences. If asked "how many people did you train?", have an honest number ready across the workshops, virtual sessions and rollouts — a soft "hundreds" or a specific figure both work; a vague "many" doesn't.

Language: AIA panels commonly switch between English, Cantonese and Mandarin depending on interviewer. Prepare the same beats in Cantonese; if the panel includes a Mainland/regional interviewer, be ready to hold at least basic exchanges in Mandarin.

The seniority-band conversation: if a panel signals "you look a bit senior for GCB05" — acknowledge, don't argue. "I'd rather earn the next step through impact at AIA than optimise for title on the way in. This is the domain and scale I want to be in for the next several years."

ASK THESE

Smart questions for the end

Ordered roughly by impact. Two or three well-chosen ones beat all eight.

Q1
"What are the two or three GIO initiatives that this role would most likely lead change management on in year one — and how mature is each in the pipeline?"
The single most useful question: it defines your first six months and proves you think in delivery portfolios rather than abstract strategy.
Q2
"For the five markets in this role's scope, how is change management currently organised — do HK, China, Singapore, Malaysia and Thailand each have their own change leads, and how does the Group Innovation Office partner with them?"
Shows you know the scope, and asks a mature question about the operating model you'd inherit.
Q3
"What's the biggest gap the team is trying to close by hiring this role — is it capacity, a capability the team doesn't have today, or something structural about how change is being run?"
Direct, mature, and the answer tells you what you'd actually be measured on.
Q4
"Post-TDA, how does innovation adoption maturity vary across the five in-scope markets — is Singapore ahead of Malaysia, is Hong Kong different from China again, and how do you calibrate rollouts to that variance?"
Names the markets specifically, asks a nuanced maturity question, and signals you know TDA is done and you're asking about now.
Q5
"For a genAI initiative touching customer-facing agent interactions, how does governance work between GIO, Legal & Compliance and local market regulators — particularly given China's NFRA context and data-residency constraints look materially different from the other four?"
Domain-specific, regulator-aware, and specifically flags China as its own category — a level of nuance that separates you from candidates who lump "Asia" into one bucket.
Q6
"What does success look like for someone in this role at the twelve-month mark — what will the Associate Director and I be pointing to as evidence it's working?"
Forward-looking and performance-oriented — surfaces how they think about impact.
Q7
"Prosci/ADKAR is called out as an advantage — is that because the team formally uses it, or is it more that they want familiar vocabulary?"
Confidently addresses the cert gap, and their answer tells you whether to prioritise the cert or just the vocabulary.
Q8
"How much of the role is Hong Kong-based delivery versus regional travel and virtual coordination with market teams?"
Practical, mature — and it's information you actually need to make a decision.
EXPECT THESE

Likely questions — and strong answers

Adapt the words; keep the shape. Every framing tip below is the interview-tested part.

Motivation · certain

"Why AIA, and why this role now?"

"Three reasons. First, the direction of my career: I've built change and governance craft across financial services and betting-industry platforms. Applying it at genuine enterprise scale — five of AIA's largest markets, five regulators, deep multi-market coordination — is the natural next chapter, and it plays to my Cantonese-Mandarin fluency in a way most roles wouldn't. Second, the timing: AIA's TDA foundation is publicly complete, and the next phase is embedding genAI into distribution and operations. Driving adoption of AI at that scale is exactly what I do today at FXCM, just smaller. Third, the mission — helping people live healthier, longer, better lives is work I'd want my career tied to."

FRAMINGThree-reason structure (career logic → market timing → mission) is easy to remember. Never lead with "my current company isn't stable" — that surfaces as flight risk. If pressed on why leave FXCM specifically, use "I've done what I set out to do there; time to apply this at a much larger canvas."
Domain gap · likely

"You don't have insurance industry experience — how do you see yourself getting up to speed?"

"Honestly: I don't. What I do have is a track record of moving into new domains and getting productive quickly — I've worked at a bank, a betting operator running exchange-like systems, a multi-asset trading group, and each shift required learning a regulated industry from scratch. My approach would be the same here: spend the first few weeks with the product teams, HR and market leads to understand the specific initiatives GIO is driving, read the regulatory landscape in each market, and shadow a Change Champion cycle before proposing changes to how it runs. Change management craft transfers; domain vocabulary and stakeholder maps are learnable in months, not years."

FRAMINGNever bluff domain knowledge — panels smell it instantly. What you're actually selling is learnable domain, transferable craft. The betting/CFD/onboarding sequence is proof, not context.
Method · likely

"Are you familiar with Prosci / ADKAR?"

"Familiar with the model — Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, Reinforcement — as a framework for individual adoption within organisational change. I don't hold the certification. My formal grounding is PMP and PSM, plus the governance disciplines I've built running PMOs. In practice much of what ADKAR names, I've been doing under different vocabulary: awareness campaigns before rollout, capability-uplift measurement during, reinforcement through governance forums after. Getting the cert would be an early first-year priority to speak the team's language cleanly."

FRAMINGThe JD says "advantage", not "required" — so acknowledge, don't apologise. Turning it into "I'll do the cert" is a small commitment that costs you nothing and neutralises the gap.
Domain · very likely

"How would you approach change management for a genAI rollout to our agent force?"

"I'd start by separating the initiative into two distinct change problems, because they need different management. First, the operational change: agents need new tools, new workflows, new success metrics — that's classic ADKAR territory, driven through the Change Champion network, with clear before/after adoption metrics and dashboards for market leads. Second, the identity change: 'AI copilot' can read as 'AI replacement' to a career agent, and if you don't manage that fear directly, adoption plateaus at surface use. So the communications strategy has to lead with augmentation stories — real agents who've grown their book with these tools — not efficiency language. Regulation is the third thread: any AI touching customer interaction faces market-level regulatory review, so the governance framework has to build in compliance sign-off per market, not centrally."

FRAMINGThree-layer answer (operational + identity + regulatory) is far more sophisticated than most candidates offer, and each layer connects to something the panel cares about. Don't oversell — end with "that's my starting hypothesis; the actual design would come after understanding the specific initiative and market."
Behavioural · likely

"Tell me about a time you drove adoption of something the business initially resisted."

Use the FXCM Claude adoption or the Salesforce/JIRA re-engineering: name the resistance clearly (fear of tooling change, loss of familiar processes, unclear ROI), what you did to move it (surface concerns through a champion group, quick wins, measurable data-driven case), what shipped, and what you'd repeat versus change. Close with one concrete adoption metric.

FRAMINGResistance stories work only if you're honest about it being real resistance — not "some minor pushback we quickly overcame". Panels reward candour. And a specific number at the end ("adoption reached X% by month six") beats any adjective.
Behavioural · likely

"Tell me about influencing senior stakeholders without direct authority."

This is a direct JD phrase ("influence without direct authority") — worth a rehearsed story. Use the CFD-to-multi-asset expansion: cross-functional stakeholders you had no line authority over (legal, compliance, ops, tech, executives), how you built the coalition (governance forum, RACI clarity, quantified impact), the specific moment influence tipped a decision, and the outcome. Emphasise that authority came from evidence and reliability, not from title.

FRAMINGThe JD asked for this exact skill — deliver a specific story, not a philosophy. End on: "That's how I've built every stakeholder relationship — through consistent evidence-based delivery, not positional authority. Which is fortunate given this role has no direct reports."
Behavioural · possible

"A market team disagrees with a Group-driven change. How do you handle it?"

"First, take the disagreement seriously — market teams usually have context Group doesn't. Separate the what from the how: is the objection to the change itself, or to the way it's being rolled out locally? Often it's the second, and adapting the how (timing, sequencing, local customisation) unlocks the disagreement without diluting the change. If it's the first — genuine strategic disagreement — that gets escalated with a clear recommendation, not a problem, so the Associate Director can decide with full context. What I wouldn't do is override a market team quietly; that erodes trust and makes the next change harder."

FRAMINGThis tests whether you'll be a "Group person" who steamrolls markets or a "change person" who protects both sides of the relationship. Answer as the second, always.

HOW TO USE THIS IN THE ROOM

The honest framing that keeps you credible

Public information runs one to three years behind internal reality — you almost certainly don't know what AIA has shipped this quarter. So instead of asserting the current state, position everything you've read as the picture from the outside, and use it to earn the right to ask about the inside. For example: "From the public record, I can see AIA's Copilot deployment in Singapore and Thailand customer service, and the stated direction toward agent-facing genAI copilots. I'd be curious where the GIO's current focus actually is — because from the outside it's not obvious whether the change management priority now is scaling those customer-service deployments to other markets, or driving the next wave of agent-copilot adoption." That framing turns your research into a well-formed question, not a claim.

This move — "here's what the public sees, tell me what's really happening" — is what senior candidates do. It respects the panel's inside knowledge while proving you've done yours.

AIA's distribution is anchored on its Premier Agency (a professional, tiered agent force) with bancassurance partnerships and direct/digital channels layered on. Any innovation that touches distribution — agent tools, sales enablement, lead management — has to work through this model.

Five regulators, one initiative — and China is a category apart

Each of the five markets is regulated separately: HK IA, China NFRA, MAS (Singapore), BNM (Malaysia), OIC (Thailand). THE AI ANGLE

AI use cases that are actually relevant to AIA

Your differentiator: you run enterprise AI adoption for a living. Speak as a practitioner about specific insurance use cases with concrete adoption challenges — not as a futurist.

01 · DISTRIBUTION

GenAI copilots for the agent force

The publicly stated flagship: AI assistants that give agents real-time customer-need analysis, product recommendations, financial advice drafts and personalised client-ready proposals — turning hours of preparation into minutes.

Adoption challenge worth naming: an agent's identity is built on client relationship expertise. A copilot has to be positioned as amplifying that expertise, or adoption stalls at superficial use. That's a change management problem, not a technology problem.
02 · UNDERWRITING

AI-assisted risk assessment

ML models flagging risk factors, drafting underwriting rationale, prioritising cases needing human review — freeing underwriters to focus on complex cases. Publicly, AIA has been piloting AI in underwriting for years.

Adoption challenge: regulatory audit trail and explainability. Underwriters need to defend every decision — so tooling has to make its reasoning visible, and the change programme has to train people to read and validate model output, not just accept it.
03 · CLAIMS

Straight-through processing with AI triage

Automated document extraction, fraud signal detection, clustering of similar claims, drafting initial assessments — routing simple cases through same-day and escalating complex ones. AIA has publicly noted ~60% of customer transactions completed within the same day post-TDA.

Adoption challenge: claims handlers can perceive automation as job threat. Change communications must frame it as "we're removing the boring 60%, so you can be the expert on the 40% that matters".
04 · CUSTOMER SERVICE

Conversational AI + agent handoff

LLM-powered chat for policy queries, claim status, product information — with smart handoff to human agents for complex or emotionally sensitive cases (bereavement, disputes, complaints).

Adoption challenge: getting the handoff threshold right. Too eager to hand off, no efficiency gain; too reluctant, brand damage. Requires ongoing tuning and joint governance between customer service and tech.
05 · MARKETING & CONTENT

Personalised marketing at scale

GenAI producing localised, segment-tailored marketing content across markets and languages — reducing the content-production bottleneck that limits campaign responsiveness. For this role's five-market scope alone that spans English, Cantonese, Mandarin (Simplified & Traditional), Bahasa Malaysia and Thai.

Adoption challenge: brand voice consistency and regulatory compliance in each market. Governance framework has to include human review gates by market before public content ships.
06 · INTERNAL PRODUCTIVITY

Enterprise copilots for knowledge work

The horizontal use case — Copilot/Claude/similar tools embedded across finance, HR, legal, ops teams for drafting, summarising, analysing. Highest volume of users, most measurable productivity impact, most direct read across from your current FXCM work.

This is the specific answer to "what would you focus on first?" — internal productivity is high volume, lower regulatory risk, gives the GIO measurable early adoption wins to build credibility for the harder distribution and customer-facing use cases.
THE GOVERNANCE THROUGH-LINE

The one insight that ties them all together

Every AI use case in insurance shares three common change challenges: regulatory acceptance (can the model's decision be defended to a regulator or in a dispute?), role identity (does this augment or replace the human doing the work today?), and market variance (compliance and cultural context differ by market — one framework, eighteen local adaptations). A Group Innovation Office's change management value is bringing consistent, measurable answers to those three questions before each pilot, not after. That framing shows up in adoption rates.

If you say one thing on AI in the interview, make it this framing. It moves you from "person who knows AI use cases" to "person who thinks about AI adoption at enterprise scale" — which is what they're hiring.
